Reflection 

The development of the University Parking System use case provided an overall understanding of how system requirements can be translated into actors, interactions, and defined processes. Identifying the key actors was relatively straightforward, as the Customer, Parking Office, and Parking Lot each have a distinct role within the system itself. Once the actors were established, the primary interactions and responsibilities within the parking process became easier to define. 

The more challenging aspect was determining the appropriate level of detail for the initial use case diagram. Including every system requirement as a separate use case would have made the initial design unnecessarily complex and difficult to follow. Organizing the requirements into broader processes helped distinguish the primary system functions from the more specific details that could be documented within an individual use case. Developing the normal and alternative flows was particularly helpful in addressing the different parking lot structures without creating separate processes for each variation. For example, entry-only lots and entry-and-exit lots could be represented within the same overall parking process while maintaining their different charging requirements. 

A primary implementation decision was to keep the initial design focused on the core functions required for parking transactions. Use Parking Lot was selected as the individual use case because it incorporates permit validation, parking fees, different scanning methods, the compact-car discount, and customer account charges. Separating these broader functions from specific rules also created a clearer structure for the system. This approach establishes a simplified foundation while still accounting for the primary requirements and variations associated with how customers use the University parking lots.
